Shivering, nauseous, and seeing my breath for the first time, I felt my body freeze the moment I stepped outside in Belgium. For someone from a tropical country, the extreme cold was a shock. It was not just uncomfortable, it felt physically overwhelming. In that moment, weather stopped being just numbers on a screen and became a lived experience. It was a time when I experienced how climate can affect our health.

<p style="margin:0.5rem 0;"></p>

As a baseline, we start with a purely statistical mortality model that captures patterns across age, time, and season, but does not incorporate any external drivers. Let $D_{x,t,w,r}$ denote the total number of deaths in region $r$ for age group $x$ during ISO week $w$ of year $t$, and $E_{x,t,w,r}$ the corresponding exposure-to-risk. We assume that the deaths follow a negative binomial distribution:

\begin{equation}
D_{x,t,w,r} \sim \text{NB}\Big(E_{x,t,w,r} \cdot \mu_{x,t,w,r}, \phi_{x,r}\Big),
\end{equation}

where $\mu_{x,t,w,r}$ is the weekly mortality rate and $\phi_{x,r}$ is the dispersion parameter. The weekly seasonal model is,

\begin{equation}
\ln(\mu_{x,t,w,r}) = \alpha_{x,r} + \beta_x \kappa_{t,r} + \gamma_x \lambda_{w,r},
\end{equation}

where $\alpha_{x,r}$ represents baseline mortality for age $x$ in region $r$, $\kappa_{t,r}$ captures long-term trends, and $\lambda_{w,r}$ represents seasonal variation. Age-specific coefficients $\beta_x$ and $\gamma_x$ represent responsiveness to these effects. While this model captures statistical patterns in mortality, it is limited because it does not account for exogenous factors such as climate, which can drive short-term deviations and delayed effects in mortality.

After fitting the baseline weekly mortality model, we looked at the residuals to capture weeks when mortality was unusually high or low. We define the log-mortality residuals as

\begin{equation}
R_{x,t,w,r} = \log\left(\frac{D_{x,t,w,r}}{E_{x,t,w,r}}\right) - \log \hat{\mu}_{x,t,w,r},
\end{equation}

where $D_{x,t,w,r}$ and $E_{x,t,w,r}$ are the observed deaths and exposure, and $\hat{\mu}_{x,t,w,r}$ is the baseline prediction. These residuals show the deviations from expected mortality, representing mortality model residuals that may be influenced by climate.

<p style="margin:0.5rem 0;"></p>

To account for climate-driven deviations in mortality, we model the log-mortality residuals using a combination of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks. The CNN extracts short-term patterns from sequences of weekly climate covariates, such as temperature, humidity, and air pollution. For each layer, a 1D convolution is applied, followed by a non-linear activation and normalization, producing feature maps that summarize local temporal correlations. The LSTM then takes these CNN features as input and models longer-term dependencies in the residuals, learning how past climate patterns influence future mortality deviations.

### Convolutional Neural Network

Convolutional Neural Networks are deep learning models designed to process structured data, such as images or time-series sequences, by learning hierarchical feature representations. Unlike fully connected networks, CNNs exploit spatial or temporal locality, making them particularly effective for detecting local patterns in structured data. The core component of a CNN is the convolutional layer, which applies learnable filters (kernels) to the input. These filters slide across the data to produce feature maps, capturing correlations between neighboring values. For 1D CNNs, suitable for weekly climate sequences, a kernel of size 3 extracts local patterns, such as short-term trends in temperature or pollutant levels. After convolution, activation functions introduce non-linearity. The Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) outputs positive values unchanged and sets negative values to zero, $\operatorname{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$, allowing the network to model complex relationships while avoiding vanishing gradients. To stabilize training and improve generalization, CNN layers are typically followed by batch normalization, which normalizes inputs across mini-batches, and dropout, which randomly deactivates neurons during training. Each convolutional layer in our model performs 1D convolution, followed by ReLU activation, batch normalization, and dropout. In equations:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{Z}^{(k)} &= \mathbf{H}^{(k-1)} \ast \mathbf{W}^{(k)} + \mathbf{b}^{(k)} \\
\mathbf{A}^{(k)} &= \operatorname{ReLU}(\mathbf{Z}^{(k)}) \\
\mathbf{B}^{(k)} &= \operatorname{BatchNorm}(\mathbf{A}^{(k)}) \\
\mathbf{H}^{(k)} &= \operatorname{Dropout}_{0.1}(\mathbf{B}^{(k)}),
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{H}^{(0)} = \mathbf{X}$ represents the input sequence, $\ast$ denotes 1D convolution, and $\mathbf{W}^{(k)}, \mathbf{b}^{(k)}$ are the kernel weights and biases. The number of filters decreases progressively to compress information while retaining relevant patterns. The output of the CNN, $\mathbf{H}^{(\text{final})}$, captures temporal-spatial features representing climate-driven patterns relevant to mortality residuals. These features are then input to recurrent layers for sequence modeling.

## Long Short-Term Memory

While the CNN captures short-term temporal patterns from fixed-length windows, it does not explicitly model longer-range dependencies. To capture longer-term sequential effects we employ a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, which maintains a memory cell $\mathbf{c}_t$ and gating variables to regulate information flow. Let $\mathbf{x}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d$ be the input at time $t$, and $\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\mathbf{c}_{t-1}$ the previous hidden and cell states. The LSTM updates are:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{f}_t &= \sigma\big(\mathbf{b}^{(f)} + \mathbf{W}^{(f)}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{V}^{(f)}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\big),\\
  \mathbf{i}_t &= \sigma\big(\mathbf{b}^{(i)} + \mathbf{W}^{(i)}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{V}^{(i)}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\big),\\
  \mathbf{o}_t &= \sigma\big(\mathbf{b}^{(o)} + \mathbf{W}^{(o)}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{V}^{(o)}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\big),\\
  \mathbf{r}_t &= \tanh\big(\mathbf{b}^{(r)} + \mathbf{W}^{(r)}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{V}^{(r)}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\big),\\
  \mathbf{c}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \mathbf{r}_t,\\
  \mathbf{h}_t &= \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tanh(\mathbf{c}_t),
\end{align}
where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid function, $\odot$ the element-wise product, and $\mathbf{W}^{(\cdot)},\mathbf{V}^{(\cdot)},\mathbf{b}^{(\cdot)}$ are learnable parameters.
