Shivering, nauseous, and seeing my breath for the first time, I felt my body freeze the moment I stepped outside in Belgium. For someone from a tropical country, the extreme cold was a shock. It was not just uncomfortable, it felt physically overwhelming. In that moment, weather stopped being just numbers on a screen and became a lived experience. It was a time when I experienced how climate can affect our health.

<p style="margin:0.5rem 0;"></p>

As a baseline, we start with a purely statistical mortality model that captures patterns across age, time, and season, but does not incorporate any external drivers. Let $D_{x,t,w,r}$ denote the total number of deaths in region $r$ for age group $x$ during ISO week $w$ of year $t$, and $E_{x,t,w,r}$ the corresponding exposure-to-risk. We assume that the deaths follow a negative binomial distribution:

\begin{equation}
D*{x,t,w,r} \sim \text{NB}\Big(E*{x,t,w,r} \cdot \mu*{x,t,w,r}, \phi*{x,r}\Big),
\end{equation}

where $\mu_{x,t,w,r}$ is the weekly mortality rate and $\phi_{x,r}$ is the dispersion parameter. The weekly seasonal model is,

\begin{equation}
\ln(\mu*{x,t,w,r}) = \alpha*{x,r} + \beta*x \kappa*{t,r} + \gamma*x \lambda*{w,r},
\end{equation}

where $\alpha_{x,r}$ represents baseline mortality for age $x$ in region $r$, $\kappa_{t,r}$ captures long-term trends, and $\lambda_{w,r}$ represents seasonal variation. Age-specific coefficients $\beta_x$ and $\gamma_x$ represent responsiveness to these effects. While this model captures statistical patterns in mortality, it is limited because it does not account for exogenous factors such as climate, which can drive short-term deviations and delayed effects in mortality. After fitting the baseline weekly mortality model, we looked at the residuals to capture weeks when mortality was unusually high or low. We define the log-mortality residuals as

\begin{equation}
R*{x,t,w,r} = \log\left(\frac{D*{x,t,w,r}}{E*{x,t,w,r}}\right) - \log \hat{\mu}*{x,t,w,r},
\end{equation}

where $D_{x,t,w,r}$ and $E_{x,t,w,r}$ are the observed deaths and exposure, and $\hat{\mu}_{x,t,w,r}$ is the baseline prediction. These residuals show the deviations from expected mortality, representing mortality model residuals that may be influenced by climate.

<p style="margin:0.5rem 0;"></p>

To capture climate-driven deviations we model log-mortality residuals with a CNN–LSTM. The CNN compresses short windows of weekly climate covariates (temperature, humidity, pollution) into compact feature maps using 1D convolutions, nonlinear activations and normalization; these maps summarize local temporal structure. The LSTM then consumes the CNN features and learns longer-range dependencies and delayed effects, so the model can relate past climate to future residuals. Formally, at each time step $t$, the LSTM updates its hidden state $\mathbf{h}_t$ and cell state $\mathbf{c}_t$ through gating mechanisms:

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{f}_t &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}^{(f)} \mathbf{x}\_t + \mathbf{V}^{(f)} \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}^{(f)}), \\
    \mathbf{i}_t &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}^{(i)} \mathbf{x}\_t + \mathbf{V}^{(i)} \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}^{(i)}), \\
    \mathbf{o}_t &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}^{(o)} \mathbf{x}\_t + \mathbf{V}^{(o)} \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}^{(o)}), \\
    \mathbf{c}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tanh(\mathbf{W}^{(c)} \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{V}^{(c)} \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}^{(c)}), \\
    \mathbf{h}_t &= \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tanh(\mathbf{c}\_t),
\end{align}

where $\mathbf{x}_t$ is the CNN-derived feature vector at time $t$. By mapping past climate sequences to residuals, the network captures short-term shocks and lagged responses and thereby improves the baseline forecasts.
